A service business runs on four tools that don't talk.

A one-van window cleaning company still has to quote, schedule crews, track hours, bill, collect and keep the books. Usually that means a paper diary, a typed estimate, a card reader and an evening re-entering everything into QuickBooks.

The usual way

  • Recurring customers live in a diary, and someone has to remember who is due this month
  • Estimates are typed from scratch and emailed as attachments
  • Crews get the day's jobs by phone call or text
  • Invoices are written one by one after the work is done
  • Every sale is entered a second time in the accounting software

With The Customer Factor

  • Recurring and one-time jobs wait in a job pool and are dragged onto the schedule
  • Estimates come from templates and go out by email or text
  • Jobs are assigned to an employee or a whole crew and show up in their app
  • Invoices are created in bulk; saved cards can pay recurring work automatically
  • Sales flow into QuickBooks Online overnight, or Desktop in a few clicks

Quote, schedule, invoice and sync, without retyping.

The Customer Factor, made by The Customer Factor LLC of Sioux Falls, South Dakota, keeps a service company's customers, jobs, prices and payments in one record. The office plans the week on the web, crews work from iOS and Android apps, and customers can request their next visit through a portal.

It's tuned for businesses whose revenue is mostly repeat visits to the same addresses, rather than for large field-service fleets.

How a job works, start to finish.

One window-cleaning customer, followed through the product.

  1. Send the estimate

    The office picks a template, adjusts the line items and sends it to the customer by text or email.

  2. Put it in the job pool

    Once approved, the work is saved as a recurring job, every 4, 6 or 8 weeks, or as a one-time visit.

  3. Drag it onto the schedule

    When it's due, the job is dragged onto a day and assigned to an employee or a crew.

  4. Do the work and track the time

    The crew sees the job in the iOS or Android app and tracks time against it on site.

  5. Invoice and get paid

    The job is invoiced in bulk with the rest of the week, and charged to a saved card if there is one.

  6. Land in the books

    The sale reaches QuickBooks Online at 3am the next morning, or Desktop on the next manual sync.

Technically, what makes it interesting.

The hard parts of this kind of product aren't the screens. They are the rules underneath them.

Recurring work as data, not reminders

A window round repeats every 4, 6 or 8 weeks. Storing frequency, service plans and pricing on the job itself is what lets the job pool fill itself.

Two accounting integrations, one ledger

A scheduled cloud push for QuickBooks Online and a user-triggered sync for Desktop. Both must agree on customers, items and payments, and never post twice.

One record, three audiences

Owners, crews and end customers see the same job with different permissions. The portal shows only what the owner allows: days, services, history and prices.

Two roads into the same ledger

Plenty of small businesses still run QuickBooks Desktop while others have moved to Online, so the product supports both, and the sync is included in the subscription.

Where a next version could go further

  • Navigation and search. The most common complaint in reviews is that records are hard to find.
  • Route optimization. Independent guides note it isn't built in, which matters once a business runs several crews.
  • Message delivery. One reviewer reported client emails not always arriving, a case for delivery tracking.
